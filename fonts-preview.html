Body Serifs

The font users will read their own writing in. Must be beautiful at 16–18px, work for long-form reading, and feel like ink on paper — not "a Word document."

Literata SIL OFL · Google Fonts · Variable ⭐ Top recommendation

The quiet hum of the page

Literata was designed by TypeTogether for Google Play Books — a font built from the ground up for long-form reading on screens. It has a slight humanist warmth without being decorative, and renders beautifully at body-text sizes. The variable font axes let it scale gracefully from small captions to large headings.

It is designed to disappear. After a paragraph or two you stop noticing the typeface and start hearing the writing. That is the highest praise a body serif can receive.

Source Serif 4 SIL OFL · Adobe · Variable ⭐ Strong alternative

The quiet hum of the page

Source Serif 4 is Adobe's open-licensed transitional serif — sober, professional, deeply legible. Where Literata leans warm and humanist, Source Serif leans clean and slightly formal. If write is going to be used for resumes, reports, and business letters, this is the font that will feel most appropriate.

The drawback: it can feel a touch impersonal for fiction or journaling. A document in Source Serif looks competent. A document in Literata looks loved.

Lora SIL OFL · Google Fonts Warm, friendly

The quiet hum of the page

Lora is a calligraphic, slightly handwritten-feeling serif. It is the warmest, friendliest option in the list. Reading Lora feels like reading a letter from someone who likes you. The contrast between thick and thin strokes is more pronounced than Literata or Source Serif, which gives it personality but can feel "less serious."

Best for: journaling, fiction, personal documents. Less ideal for: legal contracts, business reports.

Spectral SIL OFL · Production Type / Google Screen-optimized

The quiet hum of the page

Spectral was designed specifically for Google Docs — meaning it was engineered for the exact use case write serves. Long-form text in a webview-rendered document editor. It is in the same lineage as Literata but slightly more geometric, slightly less warm.

Spectral is the safest, most "Google Docs-like" choice. That is both its strength and its limitation: it will feel familiar, but it will not feel like a distinct identity.

Crimson Pro SIL OFL · Google Fonts · Variable Garamond-adjacent, modernized

The quiet hum of the page

Crimson Pro is a modern Garamond-inspired serif — old-style proportions, refined for current screens. It carries a literary, almost academic feel without the readability problems of true classical Garamonds.

If Literata feels "today" and Source Serif feels "professional," Crimson Pro feels "literary." Best for: novelists, essayists, anyone whose document might be mistaken for a book.

EB Garamond SIL OFL · Free Classical — high contrast

The quiet hum of the page

EB Garamond is a faithful revival of Claude Garamont's 16th-century type. It is, without exaggeration, one of the most beautiful serifs ever made. It also has the highest stroke contrast in our list, which means: gorgeous at large sizes, slightly fragile at small ones.

Best for: books, long essays, anything where the reader will spend significant time. Worst for: small UI text, dense paragraphs at 14px or below.

Vollkorn SIL OFL · Free Sturdy, rustic

The quiet hum of the page

Vollkorn (German for "whole grain") is a robust, slightly rustic serif. It is unfussy, has good weight, and reads well at small sizes. Compared to Literata or Source Serif, it has more visible "personality" in the letterforms.

It will not be everyone's choice but it has a devoted following. Distinctive without being weird.

PT Serif SIL OFL · ParaType Workhorse — safe choice

The quiet hum of the page

PT Serif is the workhorse. It was designed to be a universal reading font — boring in the best possible way. If you want a font that will never surprise you, that will work at any size, on any screen, for any kind of document, this is it.

UI Sans-Serifs

The font for the toolbar, menus, status bar, and dialog text. Should be neutral and crisp — its job is to not compete with the writing.

Inter SIL OFL · Rasmus Andersson · Variable ⭐ Top recommendation

Inter — the modern default

Inter is the de facto modern UI typeface. Designed by Rasmus Andersson, used by Figma, GitHub, Vercel, Mozilla, and roughly half of the web's design-conscious products. It is neutral, deeply tested at small sizes, and supports nearly every Latin language plus Cyrillic and Greek.

Choosing Inter is the equivalent of choosing a black t-shirt — nobody will compliment it but nobody will fault it either, and it will work in any context.

IBM Plex Sans SIL OFL · IBM Distinctive — slight character

IBM Plex Sans — a touch of personality

IBM gave Plex away in 2017, and it has been quietly excellent ever since. It is more humanist than Inter, with slightly more idiosyncratic letterforms (the lowercase "a" and "g" particularly). For a writing app, that personality could be a feature.

Risk: if both the body serif and the UI sans have personality, they can fight each other for attention. Plex pairs best with a quieter body serif (like Source Serif or PT Serif).

Public Sans SIL OFL · US Web Design System Neutral, civic-feeling

Public Sans — strict neutrality

Public Sans is a fork of Libre Franklin maintained by the US government's Web Design System. It is designed to be neutral, accessible, and legally unencumbered. Slightly more "official" feeling than Inter — appropriate for an app meant for serious documents.

Excellent at small sizes. Boring at large sizes. That tradeoff may be fine for UI chrome.

Manrope SIL OFL · Variable Modern, geometric

Manrope — geometric modernism

Manrope is rounder, more geometric, more "tech startup" feeling than Inter. If write wants to feel like a young product (think: Notion, Linear, Vercel), Manrope leans into that. If it wants to feel like a serious writer's tool, Manrope is too playful.

Atkinson Hyperlegible SIL OFL · Braille Institute Accessibility-first

Atkinson Hyperlegible — designed for low vision

Atkinson was designed by the Braille Institute specifically to be readable for people with low vision. The letterforms exaggerate distinctions (lowercase "l" vs uppercase "I", "0" vs "O"). It has real character — distinctive, slightly quirky.

Using it as the UI font would be a statement about accessibility-first design. It is not the most neutral choice but it is the most ethically interesting one.

My Recommendation

Body: Literata — built for screen reading, slight humanist warmth, professional but loved. Source Serif 4 is the strong alternative if you want a more formal/business feel.

UI: Inter — the modern default, neutral, never wrong. IBM Plex Sans if you want more personality, but only paired with a quieter body serif.

The pair I would ship with on day one: Literata + Inter. It is the pair Google Books and the best modern design systems already use; you would not be reinventing the wheel, but you would be using the best wheel that exists.
